# The finding

Draft 2, 2026-08-27. Supersedes Draft 1, which was built on a 12-post-per-brand window
and got the central judgement about State Life wrong. What changed and why is recorded in
§7 — the error is instructive, not incidental.

Evidence: **288 posts, 24 per brand, 12 brands.** `deep/capture.xlsx`; creative in
`deep/media/`; contact sheets in `deep/contact/`; tables in `deep/scan_findings.md`.

## 1. What we looked at

The 24 most recent posts from each brand's public Instagram, collected 27 August 2026 —
the maximum a logged-out browser will serve. Every artwork downloaded; every post opened
for its true date and full caption.

Four rings: **State Life** (client); **EFU, Jubilee, Adamjee, IGI, Askari, TPL** (direct);
**Pak-Qatar, Dawood, EFU Window Takaful** (adjacent); **Al Meezan, UBL Funds**
(substitute). Postal Life and the Central Directorate of National Savings have **no
Instagram presence at all** — recorded as absences, which is itself a finding about where
the category competes.

**Honest limits, stated before the findings, not after:**

- **Share of voice is not measurable here.** Equal N per brand is a collection artefact.
  No share-of-voice number appears in this document or the deck.
- **Everything is organic owned content.** No spend, no reach, no paid creative. Only the
  Meta Ad Library shows paid, and it publishes no spend for commercial advertisers.
- **79 of 288 posts carry no caption** and are coded to a brand-typical default rather
  than an individual read. They are labelled `claude-brand-default` in the sheet.
- **Audience-generation codes are inference from casting**, never a claim about who buys.

## 2. How the category talks

**It has converged on apps and stopped proving anything.**

Jubilee drives **79% of its output** into Jubilee Active — 19 of 24 posts are discounts,
step-counts and partner offers. EFU is at 50% across Thrive, PRIMUS and WIN. Adamjee 33%
into its Customer Care App. The claims differ brand to brand; the destination is
identical. A message-territory map cannot see this, which is why it was invisible until
`product_focus` was coded.

**Four brands published no brand-building at all** across 24 posts each — Adamjee, EFU
Window Takaful, Pak-Qatar and TPL are running product features, PR and calendar content
exclusively.

**Faith is the most crowded reassurance territory** (25 ads) and **national trust is
almost entirely decorative** — 22 of 29 national-trust ads sit in `pride`: flags, cakes,
Independence Day, a fighter jet. Seven give a reason to buy anything.

**35 of 66 claim/register combinations are unoccupied.** The `fear` column holds two ads in
288, in a category that sells against death and illness.

## 2a. Why the category is doing this

The pattern is app convergence. The mechanism behind it is distribution, and it is
documented rather than inferred.

**None of these insurers owns its customer.** Private life insurance in Pakistan sells
overwhelmingly through bancassurance: the bank holds the customer, the data and the
renewal conversation, and the insurer holds a policy number.

**The bank also rations the shelf.** The Competition Commission of Pakistan's *State of
Competition Report 2025* found that banks *"impose additional internal limits on the
amount of business insurance companies can conduct through banks"* — which the Commission
characterises as a refusal to deal. Growth through the channel is capped by somebody
else's decision.

**And the channel carries a trust problem the insurer pays for.** The same report found
that bank and insurer staff *"do not properly guide the customers about the details of the
insurance products"*, with terms presented in fine print. The insurer absorbs the
reputational cost of a sale it did not make.

**Meanwhile almost nobody has bought the product at all.** Only **3% of Pakistanis** hold
a life policy. Insurance penetration is **0.67% of GDP** against a global average of 6.7%;
density is **US$14 per head** against India's US$82. Acquisition is brutally hard, so
retention becomes the whole game.

Seen against that, the app is rational. It is a direct customer relationship the bank
cannot gate, a reason to open something between annual premium notices, and a way to make
an unwanted category feel like a wanted one.

> **But it solves the insurer's distribution problem, not the customer's trust problem.**
> Nothing in a discount voucher addresses why 97% of the population has never bought the
> product.

**This matters for State Life specifically.** State Life holds **55% of the life insurance
market** (CCP, 2025) and distributes through a tied agency force rather than through
banks. It already owns the customer relationship the others are spending their output
trying to buy. The app strategy solves a problem State Life does not have — which is the
structural reason not to copy it, rather than a matter of taste.

*Sources: Competition Commission of Pakistan, State of Competition Report 2025
(cc.gov.pk/home/viewpressreleases/613); IAP–PSOA International Insurance Conference,
July 2026.*

## 2b. Who the category is talking to

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 35% | 31% | 23% | 10% | **1%** |

The work is cast overwhelmingly for the banked, urban, app-owning customer — which is

**Two advertisements in 288 are cast for anyone over 60, and both are State Life's.** The

## 2c. Three readings that change the brief

**The category advertises in English.** Measured on the raw caption text rather than
inferred: **4 of 288 posts carry any Urdu script**, three of them Pak-Qatar's and one
State Life's. Romanised Urdu adds roughly six more — Askari's *"Har khwab ki apni ek
kahani hoti hai"*, EFU's *"abhi se? Jee haan, abhi se!"*, Al Meezan's *"Azaadi sirf ek
lafz nahi"*. In a country where Urdu is the national language and a life-insurance
category has reached 3% of the population, fewer than one post in twenty uses it.

This follows from the distribution point in §2a: English-first work addresses the banked,
urban, English-literate customer bancassurance already reaches. It is a language choice
that inherits an audience rather than choosing one.

## 5. What State Life can own

**State Life is already the only brand in the category proving anything systematically.**
The deep read shows:

- **Policyholder's Voices** — named claimants, Sara Rizvi (Islamabad) and Mahin Khan
  (Karachi), telling their own stories. A testimonial series, not a one-off.
- **E-Kachehri published twice, three weeks apart**, each time with the resolution figure
  on the creative. That is a rhythm.
- **"Trusted by Your Parents. There for Your Children. Securing Pakistanis' Future Since
  1972."** — a 54-year heritage claim.
- **"Aye Khuda Meray Abbu Salamat Rahain"** — a line that has run for five decades,
  revived for Father's Day.

> The problem was never that State Life has nothing to say. It is that these four things
> are not a campaign. They are four unrelated posts among twenty-four, with no line
> connecting them, no cadence, and no reason for anyone to notice.

**The vacant territory is proof** — not trust as a feeling, but an audited, publishable,
repeatable number. State Life can own it on three assets no private competitor can copy:

1. **Sovereign backing.** The promise is underwritten by the state. Pak-Qatar advertises
   its Federal pension-fund appointment and EFU its Federal Hemayah association — both are
   borrowing exactly what State Life holds by constitution rather than by contract.
2. **A statutory grievance obligation.** E-Kachehri is a duty, not a marketing programme.
   It generates the number every cycle whether or not marketing wants it — which is why
   the cadence is credible in a way a competitor's annual figure is not.
3. **A line nobody else could use.** *Aye Khuda Meray Abbu Salamat Rahain* carries fifty
   years of equity that cannot be bought or borrowed.

And the audience is unoccupied: the only two ads in 288 aimed at the over-60s are State
Life's own, in the segment for whom *"will they actually pay"* is least abstract.

**Do not chase the app.** Jubilee at 79%, EFU at 50% and Adamjee at 33% are competing on
retention mechanics. State Life has no ecosystem to defend and does not need one — an
insurer underwritten by the state competes on the promise, not on discount vouchers.

## 6. The choice to put to the client

Owning proof means publishing numbers on a cadence, and numbers cut both ways — a bad
quarter becomes public too. That is SLIC's decision, not ours, and the pitch should
present it as a choice rather than resolve it for them:

- **Commit to the cadence** — the published resolution figure becomes the campaign's spine
  and the category's benchmark. State Life is already doing this; the change is to make it
  the point rather than a by-product.
- **Claim the territory without the cadence** — safer, weaker, and copyable within a
  quarter by anyone willing to publish first.

## 7. What changed from Draft 1, and why

Draft 1 read 12 posts per brand and concluded State Life's channel *"reads as a ministry
newsletter"* with its proof *"filed as paperwork"* and no proof device on any brand ad.

At 24 posts that is simply false. Policyholder's Voices, the second E-Kachehri, the 1972
heritage claim and the revived fifty-year line all sat just outside a fortnight's window.

Two lessons worth carrying into the next scan:

- **A recency window is not a sample of a brand.** Twelve posts is roughly two weeks for
  an active advertiser — long enough to describe what a brand did last fortnight, far too
  short to describe what it stands for.
- **The correction strengthened the case.** The argument moved from *"State Life should
  start proving"* to *"State Life already proves, better than anyone, and then hides it"* —
  which is both truer and a far better brief for a campaign.
