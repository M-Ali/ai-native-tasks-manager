# Testing a concept before you show it

Six tests. Run them before the client does. Each one has killed real work.

## 1. The swap test

Replace the client's name with the nearest competitor's. Does the line still make sense?

If it does, you have written a category truth, not a concept. *"Securing your tomorrow"*
works for every insurer alive, which is why none of them own it.

**Pass:** the sentence becomes false, absurd or unprovable with anyone else's name on it.

## 2. The asset test

Name the thing the client has that rivals structurally cannot copy. Write it in five words.

Structural assets: ownership (state-owned, mutual, family), mandate (a statutory duty),
scale (real market share), heritage (a date, a line, a founder), distribution (a tied
force, a branch network), a guarantee, a number only they can publish.

Not assets: tone of voice, "customer focus", "innovation", a colour, a font, being
"trusted" without evidence.

**Fail condition:** if the asset is a tone, a competitor adopts it next quarter and the
work becomes theirs.

## 3. The deletion test

Delete the competitive audit. Does the concept still stand?

If yes, the audit was decoration and the concept was invented independently of the
evidence. They should be one argument: the finding makes the idea inevitable, the idea
makes the finding matter.

Conversely, delete the concept. Does the audit still lead somewhere? If not, the audit was
homework.

## 4. The department test

Which department has to change for this idea to be true?

An idea that promises fast claims when claims are slow will be killed in the room by
whoever runs claims — and rightly. An idea resting on a service standard the client does
not meet is not a brave idea, it is an unpaid invoice.

**Ask early:** does this concept require the client to *do* something new, or only to
*say* something new? Both are legitimate. Only one is cheap, and the client must know
which they are buying.

## 5. The month-twelve test

Describe the campaign in month twelve.

Many ideas are launches wearing a platform's clothes. If the second wave is just the
first wave again, it is an execution, not an idea. A platform generates work: new
executions, new audiences, new proof points, without repeating itself.

Good sign: the idea has a **mechanic** — something that recurs and refreshes. A published
figure, a series, a returning character, an annual moment.

## 6. The room test

Read the proposition aloud to someone outside the category.

Do they understand what is being promised? Can they repeat it back? Do they ask a
question that reveals the idea is thinner than it looked?

Category insiders lose the ability to hear their own jargon. "Holistic financial
wellbeing solutions" survives every internal review and dies on contact with a human.

## Scoring a concept against tender criteria

Where a tender scores the concept separately from artworks, strategy and media, check
each of those has something to execute:

| Criterion | What the concept must supply |
|---|---|
| Campaign concept | The core idea, articulated in one sentence, with rationale |
| Communication strategy | An audience the idea implies, and a message architecture it supports |
| Creative artworks | Executions that visibly express the idea, in the channels the tender gates on |
| Media mix | A channel logic the idea's mechanic actually needs |

A concept that cannot feed all four is a slogan. Build backwards from this table if the
marks are split that way.

## The honesty clause

If the strongest available idea requires a commitment the client may not make — publishing
numbers on a cadence, holding a line for three years, changing a service standard —
**present it as a choice with its trade-offs, not as a recommendation you have already
resolved.**

Clients reward being given an informed decision. They resent being handed a fait accompli,
and they discover the commitment eventually either way.
