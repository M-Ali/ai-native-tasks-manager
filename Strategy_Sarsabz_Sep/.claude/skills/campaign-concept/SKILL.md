# Campaign concept

Convert a finding into an idea a client can buy and an agency can execute.

The audit tells you where the gap is. This tells you what to put in it. If you have no
audit yet, run `category-creative-scan` first — a concept built without one is a guess
with good typography, and evaluators can tell.

## The test the concept must pass

**Could a competitor run this line tomorrow?** If yes, it is not a concept — it is a mood.
The idea must rest on something the client has and rivals structurally cannot copy:
ownership, mandate, scale, heritage, distribution, a guarantee, a legal obligation, a
number only they can publish. Tone of voice is not an asset; anyone can adopt it next
quarter.

Second test: **delete the audit. Does the concept still stand up?** If yes, the audit was
decoration and the concept was invented. The two should read as one argument — the finding
makes the idea feel inevitable, and the idea makes the finding matter.

## The chain

Each step earns the next. Skipping one shows.

```
asset        what the client has that others cannot copy
   ↓
tension      the thing the audience believes or fears that blocks the category
   ↓
territory    the vacant space where the asset resolves the tension
   ↓
proposition  one sentence, in the client's voice, that only they could say
   ↓
line         the words the work carries (may be the proposition, often is not)
   ↓
executions   proof the idea travels across channels, formats and time
```

Write the chain down before writing any copy. Most weak concepts are a line that skipped
straight to the end, and it shows because they cannot answer "why us".

## Workflow

### 1. State the asset

From the audit, not from the client's self-description. What does this brand structurally
own? A state-owned insurer has sovereign backing and statutory obligations. A 1932 company
has heritage nobody can buy. A market leader has scale nobody can claim. A challenger's
asset may be that it has nothing to defend.

If the honest answer is "nothing structural", say so and build on the sharpest available
difference instead — but know that a competitor can take it, and tell the client that.

### 2. Name the tension

The category's core objection, from the audit. Not the client's business problem — the
customer's reason for not buying. In insurance it is usually *will they actually pay*; in
finance, *is my money safe*; in health, *will this work for me*.

The best concepts resolve a tension the category has been avoiding rather than answering.
Check the audit's objection-handling rate: if it is under 10%, the tension is unoccupied.

### 3. Choose the territory

Where the asset resolves the tension. The audit's territory map shows what is crowded; the
gap analysis shows what is vacant. Pick the intersection.

**Interrogate a vacant territory before taking it.** Some are empty because they do not
work — a category with no fear-based advertising may have tested it and found it backfires.
Ask why nobody is there before assuming they missed it.

### 4. Write the proposition

One sentence. In the client's voice. Only they could say it truthfully.

Test it three ways: swap in a competitor's name — does it still make sense? (It should not.)
Strip the adjectives — is there still a claim? Read it to someone outside the category —
do they understand what is being promised?

### 5. Find the line

The line is not the proposition. The proposition is the argument; the line is what the
work carries. Sometimes they are the same sentence; usually the line is shorter, warmer and
carries a second meaning.

**Look for a line the client already owns.** A dormant heritage line with decades of equity
beats a new one with none, and reviving it is a stronger recommendation than replacing it —
cheaper, faster, and it flatters the client's own history rather than implying they wasted
it. Search the audit's `campaign_platform` column and the brand's archive before writing
anything new.

Where the market's main language is not the one the category advertises in, that is a
territory in itself. Check the audit's script analysis.

### 6. Build the executions

Enough to prove the idea travels, not a full campaign. Show it working:

- across **channels** the client actually buys (check what the tender or brief gates on —
  press and broadcast accreditation means press and broadcast executions)
- across **audiences**, especially any the audit found unoccupied
- across **time** — what does month one look like, and month twelve?

An idea that only works as a launch film is not a platform.

### 7. Write the rationale

The document that makes the idea look inevitable. Structure:

```
1. What we found        one paragraph, the audit's finding
2. What it means        the tension, and why the category avoids it
3. The idea             asset -> tension -> territory -> proposition -> line
4. Why only you         the asset, and why competitors cannot follow
5. How it travels       executions across channel, audience and time
6. What it asks of you  the commitment the idea requires, stated honestly
```

Section 6 matters more than it looks. An idea that requires the client to publish numbers,
change a product, or hold a line for three years should say so. Clients reward being told
the price of an idea; they resent discovering it later.

## Where concepts fail

**The line that could belong to anyone.** "Securing your tomorrow." Delete it.

**The insight that is a fact.** "Pakistanis are family-oriented" is not an insight, it is a
census. An insight is a tension: something true, uncomfortable, and previously unsaid.

**The idea that needs the client to be different.** A concept resting on a service standard
the client does not meet will be rejected in the room by whoever owns that department.

**Borrowed distinctiveness.** A category-typical idea executed beautifully still loses to
a plain one nobody else could have made.

**The concept that ignores the gate.** On a tender, read what the criteria actually reward.
If artworks and media mix are scored separately, the concept must be built so those
sections have something to execute.

## Adapting to the brief

Where a brief specifies product, audience or objective, the concept answers it. Where it
does not — common on tenders with a missing scope section — **state the assumption on the
page** rather than leaving it implicit. Evaluators reward a clearly labelled assumption far
more than they punish it, and an unstated one reads as an oversight.

Where a territory is commercially or politically sensitive, present it as a choice with its
trade-offs rather than resolving it for the client. That is judgement, not indecision.
