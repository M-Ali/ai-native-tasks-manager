# Communication strategy

The concept is the idea. This is the argument for who it talks to, what it says to them,
in what order, and why that order.

Runs downstream of `campaign-concept` and upstream of creative and media. If there is no
concept yet, build one first — a message architecture with no idea at the top is a
taxonomy, and it produces work that is organised but inert.

## The five parts

Most tenders and briefs ask for these in this order. Each earns the next.

| Part | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Communication objective** | What must communication change? Not what the business wants. |
| **Target audience** | Who must change, who influences them, and who actually signs. |
| **Strategic approach** | How communication will do it — the route, in one sentence. |
| **Message architecture** | What we say, in what hierarchy, and what proves each claim. |
| **Communication journey** | The order the messages arrive in, and what moves someone between stages. |

## 1. The communication objective

**A business objective is not a communication objective.** "Grow premium income 15%" is a
business objective; communication cannot deliver it alone. The communication objective is
the *change in belief or behaviour* that makes the business objective possible.

Write it as: **From [what they currently believe or do] → To [what they must believe or
do].** If you cannot state the "from", you have not diagnosed anything.

Then name the **barrier** the objective clears. Barriers are usually one of five:

| Barrier | Symptom | What communication must do |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | They do not know you exist | Reach and register |
| Understanding | They do not know what it does | Explain, demonstrate |
| **Belief** | They do not think it will work | **Prove** |
| Relevance | They think it is not for them | Reframe, re-cast |
| Salience | They believe but do not act | Prompt, remind, make it easy |

Getting this wrong wastes the budget. A category with high awareness and low belief does
not need more reach; it needs proof. The audit tells you which — check the objection-handling
rate and the proof-device rate before you decide.

## 2. Target audience

Three distinct jobs, often confused:

- **Primary audience** — who must change. Define by behaviour and attitude, not only
  demographics. "Salaried men 30-45 SEC A/B" is a media buying instruction, not an audience.
- **Influencers** — who they consult. In household financial decisions this is usually a
  spouse, a parent, or an employer.
- **The decision-making unit** — who actually signs, pays and renews. In life insurance the
  buyer, the payer and the beneficiary are frequently three different people, and the ad
  usually talks to only one of them.

**Segment by what predicts behaviour**, then say why: life-stage, attitude to risk,
existing category experience (lapsed, never-bought, active), channel access (banked or not).
Demographics describe an audience; they rarely explain it.

**Check who the category has abandoned.** If the audit shows a generation or a segment that
nobody is casting for, that is a strategic option with no competition in it — but confirm
the segment is commercially real before recommending it. Unoccupied is not the same as
valuable.

State the audience insight — the thing that is true about them, uncomfortable, and useful.
Not a census fact.

## 3. Strategic approach

One sentence naming the route. It should be arguable — if no sensible person could propose
the opposite, it is not a strategy.

Common routes, named properly:

- **Proof-led** — substantiate what the category asserts. Right when belief is the barrier.
- **Category-building** — grow the category, not share. Right for a dominant leader in an
  under-penetrated market, because most of the gain accrues to the biggest player.
- **Reframing** — change the frame of reference so the product is judged differently.
- **Salience-led** — be remembered at the moment of need. Right when belief is settled.
- **Barrier-removal** — attack a specific friction: cost, complexity, eligibility, stigma.
- **Recruitment** — take share from a named competitor. Requires a real point of difference.

Say which and why, and say what you are *not* doing. A strategy that excludes nothing has
decided nothing.

## 4. Message architecture

Also called a **message house**. Three levels, and every level must be provable.

```
                    MASTER MESSAGE
        the single proposition, in the client's voice
        ┌──────────────┬──────────────┬──────────────┐
        │   PILLAR 1   │   PILLAR 2   │   PILLAR 3   │
        │  a supporting│  a supporting│  a supporting│
        │    claim     │    claim     │    claim     │
        └──────────────┴──────────────┴──────────────┘
              PROOF POINTS / REASONS TO BELIEVE
        the evidence under each pillar - facts, not adjectives
```

Rules that keep it useful:

- **Three pillars, rarely four.** More than four and none of them is memorable.
- **Every pillar needs a proof point that is a fact**, not a restatement. "Trusted" is not a
  proof point; a published figure, a mandate, a date, a scale number is.
- **Pillars must be distinct.** If two could be merged, merge them.
- **Write a positioning statement** in the standard form, because it forces precision:
  *For [audience] who [need/tension], [brand] is the [frame of reference] that [benefit],
  because [reason to believe].*
- **Vary the message by audience, not the proposition.** One proposition, several proofs.
  A strategy with a different promise per segment is three strategies.

## 5. The communication journey

The order messages arrive in, and the job at each stage.

Use a **barrier-led journey** rather than a generic funnel where the diagnosis supports it.
A standard awareness → consideration → conversion model assumes the barrier is awareness. If
the audit says belief is the barrier, the journey should read something like **Hear it →
Believe it → Check it → Act on it → Tell someone**, and each stage names its own proof.

For each stage, specify:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Job** | What must happen in the audience's head |
| **Message** | Which pillar leads |
| **Proof** | What substantiates it at this stage |
| **Channel role** | What the channel is for — not the channel list, which belongs to media |
| **Signal it worked** | How you would know, even directionally |

Channel *roles* belong here; channel *selection, weights and budget* belong to the media
submission. Keep the boundary — it is usually scored separately.

## Where communication strategies fail

**The objective that is a business target.** Communication cannot deliver premium growth by
itself. Say what it changes.

**The audience that is a media buy.** Demographics with no attitude, no behaviour and no
insight.

**The message house with adjectives in the proof layer.** "Reliable, trusted, customer-
centric" proves nothing. If it cannot be checked, it is not a proof point.

**The journey that is a funnel diagram.** Five boxes with arrows and no argument about what
moves someone between them.

**Strategy that repeats the concept.** If the strategy section could be deleted without
weakening the creative brief, it was decoration. It should tell the creative team something
the concept does not.

**No exclusions.** Name who you are not talking to and what you are not saying. That is
where a strategy becomes a decision.

## On tenders

Where the criteria list the parts explicitly — objective, audience, approach, messaging,
journey — answer them in that order and label the sections with their words. Evaluators
score against the list they wrote.

Where the brief omits product, audience or objective, **state the assumption on the page.**
An unstated assumption reads as an oversight; a labelled one reads as judgement.
